# The reinventors outside Apple: how they did it, and what lasted

**Why this note exists.** The owner wants a recipe app that feels fresh and new but is also simple, calm, clean and premium. Earlier designs came out dated, or gimmicky and cartoony. This note looks at the people outside Apple who reinvented mobile UI in the last twenty years. It asks how they made something new feel obvious, and why some ideas lasted while others turned into gimmicks.

**How to read it.** Each claim is tagged. **(primary)** means the designer's own words, or their company's own document, fetched and read. **(secondary)** means a journalist's account, or a quote I could only read inside someone else's article. **(unverified)** means I did not find a source for it. A fetch tool sometimes paraphrases. Quote marks here hold only words the tool gave as exact.

**What this note does not repeat.** Matas's background, the origin of pull-to-refresh and Brichter's Macworld lines are in `appleTradition.md`. Linear, Arc's Dustin Senos, reviewers' praise of Things, and Mela are in `modernCraft.md`. This note adds only new material.

## 1. Matías Duarte: webOS cards, then Android Holo and Material Design

**Background.** He has a BS in computer science from the University of Maryland, with extra study in fine art and art history (Wikipedia, citing Surface, **secondary**: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mat%C3%ADas_Duarte)). In college he did "studio hours, painting on these massive canvases". He came to industrial design with no training: "I'd never known anything about industrial design… So I read a lot, and I picked it up as we went" ([Surface](https://www.surfacemag.com/articles/matias-duarte-google-is-designing-our-lives/), interview, **primary quote in a profile**). His path was Danger (Sidekick), then Palm (webOS, 2007–2010), then Google (2010 to now).

**webOS (2009).** The problem was multitasking on a phone. webOS showed running apps as live "cards". You swiped between them, dragged them to reorder, and flicked one off the top of the screen to close it ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2017/09/13/the-iphone-x-reveals-why-tim-cook-was-so-mad-about-palm/), **secondary**). As he finished the card demo at CES 2009, Duarte said: "Now how's that for some real newness" (same source, **secondary**). webOS failed as a business. TechCrunch's author blames old software problems, carrier marketing and HP as an owner. Nobody blamed the card idea. TechCrunch's 2017 piece argues that the iPhone X took up these gestures, so the idea outlived its platform (same source, **secondary**).

**Android Holo, Ice Cream Sandwich (2011).** The problem was that people "needed it, but they didn't necessarily love it". He called the earlier Honeycomb release an "emergency landing" ([Android Police](https://www.androidpolice.com/2011/10/19/interview-with-android-ux-lead-matias-duarte-google-is-taking-design-a-lot-more-seriously/), excerpts of his interview, **secondary**). He said "A huge component… is the typography", and that his goal was "to create wonder… [and] to simplify people's lives" (same). In 2011 he said "We are removing all the irritants that make it not aesthetic". He also described his test method: "we take people and ask them to do tasks with as little guidance as possible" ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2011/11/07/189857/in-love-with-android-qa-with-matias-duarte/), **primary**).

**Material Design (2014).** The team started from one question: what is software made of? Their answer was a made-up material with clear rules. Google's three principles are "Material is the metaphor", "Bold, graphic, intentional" and "Motion provides meaning". The stated goal was "a single underlying system" across devices ([Material 1 guidelines](https://m1.material.io/material-design/introduction.html), **primary**). Duarte called it "a sufficiently advanced form of paper as to be indistinguishable from magic" (The Verge, quoted by [512pixels](https://512pixels.net/2014/06/what-is-software-made-of/), **secondary**).

Google's own history piece adds the team's voices ([Material Eras](https://design.google/library/material-eras), **primary**):

- Christian Robertson: "Before Material was Material, we called it Quantum Paper. (And I'll take credit for that name!)" Gigazine, translating Google's making-of video, credits the idea to Jon Wiley ([Gigazine](http://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20150529-making-material-design), **secondary**). The two claims differ.
- Robertson again: "we had to clean out the cruft", and "we leaned into Modernist principles of minimalism".
- "Paper grounded us in reality". They built paper prototypes before writing code. The motion rule was "Motion carries meaning".
- Lynn Jepsen: Material 1 "was a stake in the ground". Only "one or two" of the 20-plus components they designed were coded at first.

**What aged badly.** Our reading (not sourced): Material spread so widely that many apps looked alike. Duarte frames its success as "making something that is coherent and cohesive, without making it all identical" ([Google Design podcast page](https://design.google/library/facing-our-interfaces), **primary**). Later versions moved toward "Eclectic shapes, bold colors, lively motion". A team member says Material You "gives the design system back to users". Material's own team admits that giving app makers more freedom means "even less control" over the result ([Material Eras](https://design.google/library/material-eras), **primary**).

**Gaps.** The Verge's 2014 Material feature did not load, and the web archive copy did not load either. I found no 2009 interview where he explains why he chose cards.

## 2. Microsoft Metro and Windows Phone (Albert Shum, Mike Kruzeniski and team)

**Background.** Shum spent 12 years designing at Nike. He has "a background in engineering and architecture, and a master's degree from the Stanford Design Program" ([Network World](https://www.networkworld.com/article/725769/smartphones-from-sneakers-to-smartphones-the-man-behind-microsoft-s-windows-phone-design.html), the reporter's words, **secondary**).

**The problem and what was new.** Team member Mike Kruzeniski wrote that "the design path that Windows Mobile was on was not sustainable". So they did not copy other phones: "we didn't look towards other mobile or PC user interfaces for inspiration". Instead they studied print and transit design: Josef Müller-Brockmann and the International Style, "Massimo Vignelli's design systems for the NY Subway Map", and the studio Experimental Jetset ([Windows blog, "From transportation to pixels"](https://blogs.windows.com/windowsdeveloper/2011/02/17/from-transportation-to-pixels/), **primary**).

**Their principles, in Kruzeniski's words** (same post, **primary**):

- "Clean, Light, Open and Fast": "remove any elements in the UI that we felt were unnecessary". He also calls this "Fierce Reduction".
- "Celebrate Typography": "Type is information, type is beautiful."
- "Content, Not Chrome": "It's the content on the phone that people want, not the buttons."
- "Alive in Motion": "The transitions between screens in a UI are as important the design of the screens themselves."
- "Authentically Digital": "we try to avoid using the skeumorphic shading and glossiness". He argues: "We don't need to make an eBook look like a book."

**Shum's own words.** "We really looked at using type as a system, not just for navigation but for creating a more personal experience." And on process: "We'd create quick prototypes and get these in front of our users, get their feedback, and keep iterating on that" ([Network World](https://www.networkworld.com/article/725769/smartphones-from-sneakers-to-smartphones-the-man-behind-microsoft-s-windows-phone-design.html), **primary quotes**). Also: "This isn't about skeuomorphism… It's about shifting the paradigm" (CNet, quoted by [OSNews](https://www.osnews.com/story/26948/the-problem-with-metro/), **secondary**).

**What failed, and why.** Two separate things failed.

1. **The business.** Joe Belfiore said in 2017: "We have tried VERY HARD to incent app devs. Paid money.. wrote apps 4 them.. but volume of users is too low for most companies to invest" (his tweet, as reproduced by [BetaNews](https://betanews.com/2017/10/09/windows-phone-really-is-dead), **secondary**). The phone died for lack of apps, not lack of design.
2. **The design on the desktop.** Windows 8 took Metro to PCs and removed the Start button. That was "one of the biggest criticisms", and Windows 8.1 brought a Start button back ([CBC](https://www.cbc.ca/news/science/revised-windows-8-brings-start-button-back-1.1357012), **secondary**). A style built for phones was forced onto a mouse-and-keyboard habit that people already had.

**Our reading (not sourced).** Metro's flat, type-led style probably helped the industry's turn to flat design. I found no source for this.

## 3. Mike Matas: _Our Choice_ and Facebook Paper

**The problem.** Matas thought ordinary feeds stripped the joy out of content. Of a sunset photo posted on Facebook, he said: "it's cluttered with a bunch of blue chrome, the photo is super small and pixelated", and it "just totally sucks the soul out of the photo" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2014/02/03/paper-now-available-for-ios-in-the-us-and-it-could-be-a-facebook-replacement), **primary quote**).

**What was new: physics everywhere.** His co-founder Kimon Tsinteris wrote the engineering account ([Facebook Engineering, "Introducing Pop"](https://engineering.fb.com/2014/04/28/ios/introducing-pop-the-animation-engine-behind-paper), **primary**):

- "When I co-founded Push Pop Press in 2010, our goal was to create a realistic, physics-everywhere experience."
- They wanted to "evoke the same delightful experience of UIScrollView throughout the whole application". In other words, the whole app had to feel the way iPhone scrolling already felt.
- "If objects respond to our touches, they should also respond to the velocity of our flick."
- Capturing the gesture's velocity "is key for interrupting animations and building fluid interfaces".

**Process.** Paper's team prototyped motion in Origami, a free plug-in built on Apple's Quartz Composer. Facebook designers Drew Hamlin and Brandon Walkin made it because building moving ideas from "sets of static images" frustrated them ([Webdesigner Depot](https://webdesignerdepot.com/facebook-gives-away-next-gen-design-tool-origami-for-free), **secondary**).

**What failed.** Facebook shut Paper down in 2016. Its message said it had "tried to take the best aspects of it and incorporate them into the main Facebook app" ([TechCrunch, Josh Constine](https://techcrunch.com/2016/06/30/recycled/), quoting Facebook, **secondary**). Constine's verdict was that Paper was "unnecessary for most and too unfamiliar for those that tried it". It fell out of the top 300 US apps within a month (same, **secondary**). **The lesson (our reading).** The physics lasted: Pop was open-sourced, and spring-and-velocity motion is now common. The separate app built around novel gestures did not last.

**Gaps.** His TED 2011 transcript did not load. I found no interview where he explains Paper's shutdown.

## 4. Loren Brichter: Tweetie, pull-to-refresh, Letterpress

The origin story and his "power and simplicity" lines are in `appleTradition.md`. What is new here is how he judges his own work now.

He told the Guardian's Paul Lewis: "Smartphones are useful tools… But they're addictive. Pull-to-refresh is addictive. Twitter is addictive." And: "When I was working on them, it was not something I was mature enough to think about." Of his children: "I regret every minute that I'm not paying attention to them because my smartphone has sucked me in" (the Guardian, as reproduced by [Longreads](https://longreads.com/2017/10/10/immature-architects/), **secondary**; original at [theguardian.com](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2017/oct/05/smartphone-addiction-silicon-valley-dystopia), which would not load).

**The lesson.** A gesture can be a lasting, obvious idea and still age badly in its effect. Pull-to-refresh folded a chore into scrolling. That is good design. Its inventor now calls it "addictive". The slot-machine comparison is the commentators', not his.

**Gaps.** I found no primary source on Letterpress's flat, quiet visual design. His 2008 post on fast scrolling is on a host with a broken certificate, so it stays **unverified**.

## 5. The gesture-first apps: Clear, Tinder, Snapchat, Mailbox

**Clear (2012, Realmac with Impending: Phill Ryu and Milen Dzhumerov).** It was a to-do app with no buttons, only pulls, swipes and pinches. Ryu: "There's so much crap trying to get your attention in other to-do apps", and "with Clear it's so fast it's sometimes even fun". On buttonless design: "Have you seen babies play with an iPad?… There's nothing more natural than that on a touchscreen device" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2012/02/14/clear-why-this-simple-to-do-list-app-has-everyone-talking/), **primary quotes**). Elsewhere he called virtual buttons "about the most unsatisfying interaction you can have in a touchscreen device" (The Next Web, quoted by [SiliconFilter](https://www.siliconfilter.com/no-more-buttons-clear-demonstrates-the-power-of-a-purely-gesture-based-interface/), **secondary**). Its craft was a heat-map colour scale and a different chime for each gesture. **The weakness:** the reviewer notes that the cues "are not revealed until you start interacting with the interface", and the reviewer was "not sure if gestures alone are attractive enough on their own" ([MacStories, Cody Fink](https://www.macstories.net/reviews/clear/), **secondary**). Clear had to ship a tutorial to teach itself.

**Tinder's swipe (2012, Jonathan Badeen).** Badeen: "I was imagining how to go through stacks of cards in real life, and saw three piles: yes, no and maybe." The first version had no swipe at all. The swipe "evolved over a six month period where Badeen was fussing and tinkering with the animation" ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/2016/04/04/why-swipe-right-wasnt-in-the-first-version-of-tinder/), quote **primary**, the rest **secondary**). CNBC reports him saying "Using the buttons to move the cards felt clunky… I wanted more fluidity", and telling a story about wiping a fogged mirror. CNBC refused the fetch, so this is **reported, not checked** ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2017/01/06/how-a-tinder-founder-came-up-with-swiping-and-changed-dating-forever.html)). **Why it lasted:** it copied a physical habit, sorting cards into piles. It took six months of tuning. **What aged (our reading, not sourced):** the endless swipe invites compulsive use, the same pattern as Brichter's regret.

**Snapchat's camera-first launch (Evan Spiegel).** Spiegel told the US Senate in 2024: "We designed Snapchat to open into the camera, instead of a content feed, to encourage creativity instead of passive consumption" ([Senate testimony PDF](https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/2024-01-31_-_testimony_-_spiegel.pdf). The PDF would not extract, so this wording comes from a search excerpt and is **not checked**). **What failed:** the 2018 redesign. In an internal memo Spiegel wrote: "We rushed our redesign, solving one problem but creating many others… We slowed down our product and eroded our core product value" ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2018-10-05-snap-evan-spiegel-app-redesign-rushed.html), quoting the memo, **secondary**). The core value was speed, and a rushed change broke it.

**Mailbox (2013, Gentry Underwood, formerly of IDEO).** The insight came from research for an earlier to-do app: "people used email as a to-do list pretty terribly". A short swipe right archives, a long one deletes, and a swipe left snoozes. The aim was "an experience around, 'This I'll do quickly,' 'This I'll defer,' 'This I'm done with.'" On making the new feel obvious: "We've tried to create something that's familiar. But then when you start using it, it seems obvious." He cited Jony Ive's phrase "naively obvious". On process: "we decided to defer judgment and ask what if" ([AllThingsD](https://allthingsd.com/20130207/qa-with-mailbox-ceo-gentry-underwood-on-the-launch-of-his-much-hyped-app/), **primary**). That Mailbox was inspired by Clear is **unverified**. **What failed:** Dropbox bought Mailbox and shut it down in 2016: "we've made the difficult decision to shut down Carousel and Mailbox". TechCrunch reports that neither app was "seeing anything like sustained popularity beyond their initial releases" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2015/12/07/failing-to-find-users-dropbox-will-shut-down-mailbox-in-february-2016-and-carousel-in-march), **secondary**). So use faded first, and the change of focus followed. Our reading: swipe-to-archive and swipe-to-snooze now appear in many mail apps.

## 6. Recent reinventors of look and feel

**Arc (The Browser Company).** The most useful primary source in this note is CEO Josh Miller's own post-mortem ([Letter to Arc members, 2025](https://browsercompany.substack.com/p/letter-to-arc-members-2025), **primary**):

- "for most people, Arc was simply too different, with too many new things to learn, for too little reward." The team called this the "novelty tax".
- The data: "Only 5.52% of DAUs use more than one Space regularly." "Only 4.17% use Live Folders." A favourite feature was used by "0.4%".
- "Arc lacked cohesion — in both its core features and core value."
- Scott Forstall, who led iPhone software at Apple, told them Arc was a saxophone, "powerful but hard to learn". His advice was to make it a piano.
- The new rule: "New interfaces start from familiar ones." Their next browser aims to "hide complexity behind familiar interfaces".
- "I would've stopped working on Arc a year earlier."

**Linear.** See `modernCraft.md`.

**Things 3 (Cultured Code, Werner Jainek).** Jainek says the aim is "unrivaled simplicity". Each update is about "making the concepts we've had in the app even simpler", not adding features ([App Store story](https://apps.apple.com/us/story/id1390028519), **primary**). Ideally the app disappears while you use it: "verschwindet die App idealerweise während der Benutzung". He calls it "a very personal tool, a conversation with oneself" ([Apfelfunk](https://apfelfunk.com/apps-made-in-germany-things/), **primary**, translated by me). Apfelfunk adds that each design change is tried in several versions before it ships (**secondary**). Version 3 took about five years after version 2 ([MacStories](https://www.macstories.net/reviews/things-3-beauty-and-delight-in-a-task-manager/), **secondary**). Its one novel control, the draggable "Magic Plus" button, sits on top of an otherwise conventional list (our reading of the [MacStories review](https://www.macstories.net/reviews/things-3-beauty-and-delight-in-a-task-manager/)).

**Teenage Engineering (Jesper Kouthoofd).** He trained in graphic design: "I went to school to study graphic design… using the camera and making everything by hand" ([SFMOMA](https://www.sfmoma.org/read/stay-curious-stay-naive-an-interview-with-teenage-engineering-jesper-kouthoofd/), **primary**). His method is rules ([same source](https://www.sfmoma.org/read/stay-curious-stay-naive-an-interview-with-teenage-engineering-jesper-kouthoofd/)):

- "I set up restrictions when I design." "I stick to rules and try to understand them, because then I can become free again."
- "I always work with simple geometric shapes, RAL colors — which is a 40-color scale".
- Colour carries a fixed meaning: "If it's orange or red, it means recording." "I connect colors to a meaning and then apply that to all products."
- On play and tools: "A product can be entertaining and be a tool."
- On learning: "We thought we were reaching 99% completion and then, oh, we have a thousand percent left to do".

**Our reading.** The play reads as premium, not cartoony, because every colour and shape follows a strict rule.

**Nothing (Carl Pei; design lead Adam Bates, formerly 14 years at Dyson).** Its transparent back is a story: "as soon as you go transparent you can start to tell a story about the components within the phone". Bates objects to phones that are "slabs which don't really tell you anything about what they do" ([Dezeen 2022](https://www.dezeen.com/2022/08/19/nothing-phone-1-design/), **primary quotes**). Dezeen, in its own words, links the Glyph lights to Vignelli's subway map. That is the same source Metro used. Bates admits the retro feel is deliberate ("There is a conscious element to that"). He also says "data is going to lead us to the same place as everyone else", and "If you do too much too soon… we will not survive" ([Dezeen 2024](https://www.dezeen.com/2024/02/20/nothing-tech-smartphone-design-adam-bates-interview/), **primary**). **A warning sign:** community posts report that the dot-matrix Ndot font appears less often in Nothing OS 3, and users there suspect that some alphabets do not suit it ([Nothing Community](https://nothing.community/d/17969-phone-2a-os3-beta-discussion?page=11), **secondary, unofficial**). A signature novelty typeface may have met the limits of real use.

**Others the sources point to.** Mike Kruzeniski (Metro's principles), Christian Robertson and Jon Wiley (Material's origin), Kimon Tsinteris (Paper's physics), Scott Forstall (the piano advice), and Massimo Vignelli, whose subway map is a shared root of Metro and Nothing.

## What separates a lasting reinvention from a gimmick

1. **It removes something.** The ideas that lasted took away chrome: Brichter's refresh button, Matas's "blue chrome", Kruzeniski's "Fierce Reduction" and "Content, Not Chrome". A gimmick adds a layer instead. ([Windows blog](https://blogs.windows.com/windowsdeveloper/2011/02/17/from-transportation-to-pixels/); [TechCrunch 2014](https://techcrunch.com/2014/02/03/paper-now-available-for-ios-in-the-us-and-it-could-be-a-facebook-replacement))

2. **It extends a habit people already have.** Scrolling became refreshing. Sorting cards became Tinder's swipe. The phone's camera became Snapchat's home screen. Underwood: "familiar… then… it seems obvious." Miller: "New interfaces start from familiar ones." ([AllThingsD](https://allthingsd.com/20130207/qa-with-mailbox-ceo-gentry-underwood-on-the-launch-of-his-much-hyped-app/); [Arc letter](https://browsercompany.substack.com/p/letter-to-arc-members-2025))

3. **The payoff must beat the learning cost.** Arc's "novelty tax" is the clearest case: "too many new things to learn, for too little reward". Paper was "too unfamiliar". Forstall's advice: build a piano, not a saxophone. ([Arc letter](https://browsercompany.substack.com/p/letter-to-arc-members-2025); [TechCrunch 2016](https://techcrunch.com/2016/06/30/recycled/))

4. **One internal rule, applied everywhere.** Material's single "metaphor" with consistent physics. Kouthoofd's colour that always means the same thing. Tsinteris's velocity and interruption rule across the whole app. Consistency is what makes the new feel learnable rather than random. ([Material](https://m1.material.io/material-design/introduction.html); [SFMOMA](https://www.sfmoma.org/read/stay-curious-stay-naive-an-interview-with-teenage-engineering-jesper-kouthoofd/); [Pop](https://engineering.fb.com/2014/04/28/ios/introducing-pop-the-animation-engine-behind-paper))

5. **Borrow from a mature craft outside software.** Metro studied Swiss typography and subway signs. Nothing's lights point to the same source. Material started from paper. A deep, old craft gives a new look roots, so it reads as considered, not cartoony. ([Windows blog](https://blogs.windows.com/windowsdeveloper/2011/02/17/from-transportation-to-pixels/); [Material Eras](https://design.google/library/material-eras))

6. **Novelty in one place, convention everywhere else.** Things 3 adds one new control on top of a plain list (our reading). Arc spread novelty across Spaces, Live Folders and more, and most features stayed unused, at 5.5%, 4.2% and 0.4%. ([App Store story](https://apps.apple.com/us/story/id1390028519); [Arc letter](https://browsercompany.substack.com/p/letter-to-arc-members-2025))

7. **Every hidden gesture needs a visible way in.** Clear's cues were "not revealed until you start interacting", so it needed a tutorial. Make the gesture the fast path, not the only path. ([MacStories](https://www.macstories.net/reviews/clear/))

8. **Motion explains; it does not decorate.** "Motion provides meaning". "The transitions between screens… are as important" as the screens. Good motion responds to "the velocity of our flick" and can be interrupted. ([Material](https://m1.material.io/material-design/introduction.html); [Windows blog](https://blogs.windows.com/windowsdeveloper/2011/02/17/from-transportation-to-pixels/); [Pop](https://engineering.fb.com/2014/04/28/ios/introducing-pop-the-animation-engine-behind-paper))

9. **Tune it for months, test it with little guidance, never rush it.** Tinder's swipe took six months of tuning. Duarte tests "with as little guidance as possible". Shum's team ran "quick prototypes… keep iterating". Snap: "We rushed our redesign." ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/2016/04/04/why-swipe-right-wasnt-in-the-first-version-of-tinder/); [MIT TR](https://www.technologyreview.com/2011/11/07/189857/in-love-with-android-qa-with-matias-duarte/); [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2018-10-05-snap-evan-spiegel-app-redesign-rushed.html))

10. **Respect the user's time and attention.** Things aims to "disappear" in use. Brichter regrets that "Pull-to-refresh is addictive". A design built to hook people ages badly even when the gesture itself is good. Calm is a lasting quality. Compulsion is not. ([Apfelfunk](https://apfelfunk.com/apps-made-in-germany-things/); [Longreads/Guardian](https://longreads.com/2017/10/10/immature-architects/))

_Also note that **business failure is not design failure**. webOS died with HP and the carriers, and Windows Phone died for lack of apps. Mailbox is mixed: use faded, then Dropbox changed focus. Our reading: their cards, type-led layouts and swipe-to-triage live on in today's phones._

## What this means for a recipe app

1. **The food photo and the recipe text are the design. The chrome is not.** Matas's complaint about "blue chrome" and a "super small" photo describes the cartoony failure exactly. Remove frames, badges and coloured panels before adding anything. (Principle 1)

2. **Look outside software for the new, mature look.** Use cookbook and editorial typography the way Metro used subway signs, rather than illustration or mascots. The freshness comes from a craft tradition, so it reads as premium. (Principles 5 and 1)

3. **Spend novelty in one place.** Pick one signature moment, for example how cooking mode moves step by step, and keep lists, forms and settings conventional. A cook with wet hands pays the "novelty tax" at the worst possible time. (Principles 3 and 6)

4. **Extend habits cooks already have.** Turning a page, ticking off an ingredient, setting a timer: these are the recipe equivalents of Tinder's card piles. Make each new gesture the obvious next step of one of them, never something to learn from a tutorial. (Principle 2)

5. **Give every gesture a visible button.** Swipe-to-next-step or swipe-to-tick can be the fast path. Keep a visible control too, for discovery, accessibility and messy hands. (Principle 7)

6. **One motion rule for the whole app.** Let motion follow the finger, keep its speed, stop when touched, and show where things came from. Do not bounce things for fun. A single rule makes motion feel calm and premium. (Principles 4 and 8)

7. **Do not add engagement hooks.** No pull-to-refresh slot machine and no endless swipe feed of recipes. Let the app "disappear" while someone cooks. Calm is the premium feature. (Principle 10)

8. **Prototype the motion, test it with little help, and do not ship it rushed.** Build the signature interaction in a working prototype and tune it for weeks, not hours. Then watch people use it with little help, as Duarte does. (Principle 9)
