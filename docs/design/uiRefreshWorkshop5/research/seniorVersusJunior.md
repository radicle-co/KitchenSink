# Senior versus junior UI design: what the people who hire them say

Research note for the UI refresh workshop, 20 October 2026. It is research only. It contains no design.

**Why this note exists.** The product owner calls the team's work amateur, cartoony, dense and oversized ("designed for someone who wears reading glasses"). The team leaned on visual metaphors, and it used worst-case accessibility rules as the default look. This note collects what hiring companies, teachers and critics say separates senior work from junior work. It puts most weight on the lessons that match that complaint.

**How to read the quotes.** Text in quote marks was checked word for word against the raw page. Where I had only a search summary, the claim is tagged **[unverified]** and is written without quote marks.

---

## 1. What top companies ask for in senior, staff and principal roles

Senior job posts rarely ask for "creativity". They ask for detail, quality, restraint and systems.

- **Linear** (Senior / Staff Product Designer):
    - "Quality is our competitive advantage."
    - "we believe that craft and quality have lasting value."
    - It wants a designer who spots "smaller quality issues and "paper cuts"". It wants "robust design skills, sharp product thinking", and "Humility to accept feedback, adapt your thinking, and iterate toward the best solution".
    - Source: https://jobs.accel.com/companies/linear/jobs/69522598-senior-staff-product-designer (mirror of a closed Linear post)
- **Stripe** (Staff Product Designer, Payments/Link):
    - "8+ years of product design experience with a strong visual and mobile design foundation"
    - "Deep fluency across visual design, interaction design, and prototyping"
    - "Define the visual design direction for net-new Link products"
    - Source: https://stripe.com/jobs/listing/staff-product-designer-payments/7202630
    - Other Stripe staff posts reportedly ask for a portfolio of polished UI work, and for the designer to set the bar for design excellence. **[unverified: search summary only]**
- **Google** (Senior Staff Visual Designer, Search):
    - It wants a designer who can "weave iconography, typography, color, space and texture together".
    - It holds that "all of our products should be beautiful and accessible".
    - It prefers a "focus on detailed visual/motion design".
    - Source: https://jobs.anitab.org/companies/google-24698/jobs/49490576-senior-staff-visual-designer-search-ux-foundations (a mirror of a Google post)
- **Netflix** (Senior Product Designer, Emerging Creative):
    - "Exceptional attention to detail and a passion for maintaining high quality"
    - It asks candidates to "demonstrate exceptional visual craft".
    - The role is about "elevating the aesthetics and usability of the Netflix product experience".
    - Source: https://builtin.com/job/senior-product-designer-emerging-creative/7683617
- **Airbnb** (Staff Experience Designer, Design Language System):
    - "Excellent visual, interaction and prototyping design skills"
    - The designer is the steward for "attention to design details" and is "Responsible for advancing craft on their team."
    - Source: https://www.workingnomads.com/jobs/staff-experience-designer-dls-airbnb (a mirror of an expired post)
- **Figma** (a recruiter on how hiring managers read portfolios):
    - Reviewers have "minutes, or even seconds".
    - "Design VPs often got to where they are because they have an eye for tiny incongruencies."
    - Typos and visual slips both count against you because "They both fit into the category of being detail oriented."
    - Source: https://www.figma.com/blog/product-design-portfolio-tips-from-a-figma-recruiter/
- **Apple** (Human Interface Designer):
    - Copies of the post ask for typography, colour, layout and visual hierarchy, plus a keen eye for subtle details. **[unverified: I found only third-party mirrors, none from jobs.apple.com]**
    - Source: https://designproject.io/jobs/human-interface-designer-at-apple-cy61uw (now gone)

**What this means.** No company asks for illustration or metaphor as a senior skill. All of them ask for an eye for small faults. In Linear's redesign, the team set out to "reduce visual noise, maintain visual alignment, and increase the hierarchy and density of navigation elements". It cut back on accent colour ("limiting how much chrome (blue in our case) was used"), aiming for "a more neutral and timeless appearance". It spent time "aligning labels, icons, and buttons". And it "tested a lot of options, from very condensed to more spacious configurations." Source: https://linear.app/now/how-we-redesigned-the-linear-ui

## 2. Education and how skill is actually built

**Art schools teach type, grids and critique first.**

- **RISD:** "In the studio students learn the fundamental value of typography, imagery, grids, systems and more". Source: https://www.risd.edu/academics/graphic-design
- **ArtCenter:** "You'll begin by building a foundation in typography, image-making and systems thinking". Working professionals "bring current industry insight with them into every critique." Source: https://www.artcenter.edu/academics/undergraduate-degrees/graphic-design/overview.html
- **Royal College of Art (Visual Communication MA):** It selects on "sufficient technical skill to realise intentions" and the "ability to … engage in debate and respond to criticism". Source: https://www.rca.ac.uk/study/programme-finder/visual-communication-ma/
- **Parsons:** The programme page did not load. **[unverified]**

The shared core is type, grids, systems, and being critiqued often. None of these schools puts illustration or decoration at the centre.

**The Swiss and modernist tradition is about discipline, not decoration.** Massimo Vignelli's _Canon_ (Vignelli trained Pentagram partner Michael Bierut) says:

- "Every detail is important because the end result is the sum of all the details". And: "Design without discipline is anarchy".
- On metaphor in type: "I don't believe that when you write dog the type should bark!"
- On loudness: visual strength "should never be confused with just visual impact - which, most of the time, is just an expression of visual vulgarity and obtrusiveness."
- On how few typefaces you need: "Personally, I can get along well with a half a dozen".
- Source: https://www.rit.edu/vignellicenter/sites/rit.edu.vignellicenter/files/documents/The%20Vignelli%20Canon.pdf

Josef Müller-Brockmann is widely quoted as saying that the grid system is an aid, not a guarantee. I did not check this against the book. **[unverified]** Source: https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/341206

**Dieter Rams** says: "Good design is as little design as possible" and "Less, but better – because it concentrates on the essential aspects, and the products are not burdened with non-essentials." Source: https://www.vitsoe.com/us/about/good-design

**What top designers say built their skill.** They name looking, copying, volume of work, and growing out of cleverness.

- **Michael Bierut (Pentagram):** "Early on in my career, I was enchanted by what I now would call "cleverness"". The work he admires has "an enduring intelligence and simplicity that transcends cleverness." He says school puts "a premium … on individual performance … trying to do the thing that will really stand out", which does not prepare you for work that must last for years. Source: https://monocle.com/culture/media-industry/michael-bierut-pentagram-design-interview/
- **Erik Kennedy (Learn UI Design):** He learned by studying other people's work. "I haven't had to invent any new UI elements yet. That means I can always see how others have done it and cherry-pick from the best." His advice is to "Analyze what works." Source: https://www.learnui.design/blog/7-rules-for-creating-gorgeous-ui-part-2.html
- **Jessica Hische** started lettering at college because she had no money for good fonts, and later worked for Louise Fili. She is reported to have said that subtlety is everything. **[unverified: search summary only; the page itself did not load]** Source: https://www.designweek.co.uk/21-top-tips-from-letterer-jessica-hische/
- **Ellen Lupton:** _Thinking with Type_ is the standard text on typographic hierarchy, but I found no primary page to check a quote against. **[unverified]**
- **Bauhaus:** I tried two Bauhaus foundation pages for Gropius's 1919 manifesto. Neither loaded, so I could not trace a primary quote. **[unverified]** (Vignelli is an Italian modernist, so this note says "Swiss and modernist" rather than "Swiss".)

**Takeaway.** A degree and self-teaching lead to the same place. You get there through type, grids, and many rounds of critique, while studying the best existing work.

## 3. Visual signs of junior work versus senior work

**Hierarchy through size alone.** This is the clearest junior habit, and it matches "oversized".

- Refactoring UI says: "A common mistake when styling UI text is relying too much on font size to control your hierarchy." It adds: "try using color or font weight to do the same job." Its advice is to use two or three text colours, and "two font weights is usually enough for UI work". Source: https://medium.com/refactoring-ui/7-practical-tips-for-cheating-at-design-40c736799886
- The book's table of contents has chapters named "Hierarchy is Everything", "Size isn't everything", "De-emphasize to emphasize", "Establish a type scale", "Start with too much white space" and "Everything has an intended size". Source: https://www.refactoringui.com/
- Erik Kennedy: "Page titles are the only element to style all-out up-pop. For everything else, you need up-pop and down-pop." Large numbers on a good site are "simultaneously downplayed with a very light font-weight and lower-contrast color". Source: https://www.learnui.design/blog/7-rules-for-creating-gorgeous-ui-part-2.html

**Type scale and number of sizes.** NN/g says:

- "Use no more than 3 sizes". It gives web body text as "14px to 16px", subheads as "18px to 22px", and headers "up to 32px".
- "Limit how many elements are big to a maximum of 2".
- "Use no more than 3 contrast variations … If everything is contrasted, then nothing stands out."
- Source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/visual-hierarchy-ux-definition/

**Oversized icons.** Refactoring UI: "icons that were drawn at 16–24px are never going to look very professional when you blow them up to 3x or 4x their intended size. They lack detail, and always feel disproportionately "chunky"." Source: https://medium.com/refactoring-ui/7-practical-tips-for-cheating-at-design-40c736799886

**White space versus density.** Generous space does not mean big type.

- Kennedy's model example uses a "12px font with just as much padding above and below it". His rule is "Put space between your lines. Put space between your elements. Put space between your groups of elements." Source: https://www.learnui.design/blog/7-rules-for-creating-gorgeous-ui-part-1.html
- NN/g's bad example is a page whose elements "are all relatively equal in size and color and lack breathing room". Source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/visual-hierarchy-ux-definition/

So "dense" in the owner's sense usually means too little grouping and space, not too much information. NN/g says minimalism means "just enough elements". Source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/aesthetic-minimalist-design/

**Colour restraint.**

- Kennedy: "Add color last, and even then, only with purpose." And: "Having too many colors in too many places is a really easy way to screw up clean/simple." He notes that "cartoony" styles "need a designer who can use color extremely well", and that "most apps don't have a specific attitude except clean and simple." Source: https://www.learnui.design/blog/7-rules-for-creating-gorgeous-ui-part-1.html
- NN/g: "limit your color use to 2 primary and 2 secondary colors." Source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/visual-hierarchy-ux-definition/

**Borders, boxes and clutter.** Refactoring UI: "using too many of them can make your design feel busy and cluttered". Use a shadow, a background change or "extra spacing" instead. Source: https://medium.com/refactoring-ui/7-practical-tips-for-cheating-at-design-40c736799886

**Metaphor, gimmick and decoration.**

- NN/g: "communicate; don't decorate". It calls anything "meant for "decoration" only" noise. It also says "Every extra unit of information in an interface competes with the relevant units of information". Source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/aesthetic-minimalist-design/
- NN/g on skeuomorphism: as a trend it meant "Excessive use of real-world aspects that were not necessary to the design". It "led to cluttered interfaces" and later felt "outdated and cumbersome". Metaphor still works as a "functional learning aid", such as a folder icon. Source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/skeuomorphism/
- Paul Graham: "Designers trying to be artistic resort to swooshes and curlicues… It's all evasion." Source: https://paulgraham.com/taste.html

**Illustration overuse.** AIGA Eye on Design describes how "Corporate Memphis was at least in part born out of the desire to inject color and personality into the pallid landscape of flat design". The article traces the backlash against these cartoon mascots. Source: https://eyeondesign.aiga.org/the-internet-is-turning-on-big-techs-colorful-corporate-mascots/

**Icons without words.** NN/g: "a text label must be present alongside an icon". It adds: "Keep the design simple and schematic." Source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/icon-usability/

**Alignment and consistency.**

- Figma's recruiter praises "8 equally sized, thoughtfully crafted rectangles". Source: https://www.figma.com/blog/product-design-portfolio-tips-from-a-figma-recruiter/
- Apple warns that "Mixing too many different typefaces can obscure your information hierarchy … making an interface feel internally inconsistent or poorly designed." Source: https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/typography

## 4. How taste is described and built, and how good designers edit

**Taste is real and it can be trained.**

- Paul Graham: "If taste is just personal preference, then everyone's is already perfect". Instead, "your old tastes were not merely different, but worse." He describes how taste grows: "A novice imitates without knowing it; next he tries consciously to be original; finally, he decides it's more important to be right than original." And: "Good design is redesign." Source: https://paulgraham.com/taste.html
- Ira Glass (2009 video interview) describes the gap between a beginner's taste and their skill: "your taste is good enough that you can tell that what you're making is kind of a disappointment to you." He says you close the gap "by going through a volume of work". **[Transcript by James Clear, quoted on Kottke. Not checked against the video.]** Source: https://kottke.org/20/12/the-gap-between-having-good-taste-and-doing-good-work

**Editing your own work.**

- **Subtract, don't add.** Kennedy works in black and white first: "Start with the harder problem of making the app beautiful and usable in every way, but without the aid of color." Source: https://www.learnui.design/blog/7-rules-for-creating-gorgeous-ui-part-1.html
- **Fix the contrast, not the size.** Refactoring UI: "Making the text closer to the background color is what actually helps create hierarchy". Source: https://medium.com/refactoring-ui/7-practical-tips-for-cheating-at-design-40c736799886
- **Hold a real critique.** Figma's own team found that its critiques failed when "Feedback was too shallow", when "Feedback was not actionable", and when "People were too "nice" rather than "honest"". It fixed this with smaller rooms, timed rounds, silent written critiques, and print-outs on the wall. Source: https://www.figma.com/blog/design-critiques-at-figma/

## 5. Platform defaults: what "normal size" actually is

This is the section most relevant to the "reading glasses" complaint. Both platforms ship a standard default and let the user scale it. Neither makes the default view huge.

**Apple HIG: iOS/iPadOS Dynamic Type at the "Large (default)" setting.** Source: https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/typography

| Style       | Weight   | Size (pt) | Leading (pt) |
| ----------- | -------- | --------- | ------------ |
| Large Title | Regular  | 34        | 41           |
| Title 1     | Regular  | 28        | 34           |
| Title 2     | Regular  | 22        | 28           |
| Title 3     | Regular  | 20        | 25           |
| Headline    | Semibold | 17        | 22           |
| Body        | Regular  | 17        | 22           |
| Callout     | Regular  | 16        | 21           |
| Subhead     | Regular  | 15        | 20           |
| Footnote    | Regular  | 13        | 18           |
| Caption 1   | Regular  | 12        | 16           |
| Caption 2   | Regular  | 11        | 13           |

- The HIG's default/minimum table gives iOS and iPadOS a default of "17 pt" and a minimum of "11 pt".
- The largest standard style is 34 pt. Body only grows past 17 pt when the user chooses a larger size. At the largest accessibility size (AX5), Body is 53 pt.
- The HIG says text styles "allow text to scale proportionately when people change the system's text size or make accessibility adjustments". Using them "ensures support for Dynamic Type and larger accessibility type sizes … which let people choose the text size that works for them."
- On what users really want: "When someone chooses a larger text size, they typically want to make the content they care about easier to read; they don't always want to increase the size of every word on the screen."
- The other half is just as binding: "Make sure your app's layout adapts to all font sizes." Turn on Larger Accessibility Text Sizes "and confirm that your app remains comfortably readable." And "Maintain a consistent information hierarchy regardless of the current font size."

**Apple control size.** The HIG accessibility page gives iOS/iPadOS a "Default control size" of "44x44 pt" and a "Minimum control size" of "28x28 pt". Source: https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility. This is the tap area. A small visual element can sit inside a 44 pt hit area, so the visible shape does not need to be 44 pt.

**Material 3 baseline type scale** (Jetpack Compose defaults, size/line height). Source: https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/compose/designsystems/material3

| Role                  | Large | Medium | Small |
| --------------------- | ----- | ------ | ----- |
| Display               | 57/64 | 45/52  | 36/44 |
| Headline              | 32/40 | 28/36  | 24/32 |
| Title (Medium weight) | 22/28 | 16/24  | 14/20 |
| Body                  | 16/24 | 14/20  | 12/16 |
| Label (Medium weight) | 14/20 | 12/16  | 11/16 |

- The same page says: "Your product will likely not need all 15 default styles".
- Android sizes text in `sp`: "The sp unit is the same size as a dp, by default, but it resizes based on the user's preferred text size. Never use sp for layout sizes." Source: https://developer.android.com/training/multiscreen/screendensities

**What WCAG actually asks for.** SC 1.4.4 Resize Text requires that "text can be resized without assistive technology up to 200 percent without loss of content or functionality". The Understanding page states that "The scaling of content is primarily a user agent responsibility". It says content passes "if it can be scaled up to 200% using at least one text scaling mechanism". Source: https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/resize-text.html

**The lesson.** Accessibility is met by honouring the system text size: Dynamic Type, `sp`, and browser zoom. It is not met by shipping AX-sized text to everyone. Ship body text at the platform default (17 pt on iOS, 16 sp Body Large on Android, 14–16 px on web). Then prove the layout holds at the largest sizes. Both halves are required.

## The 15 most useful rules a designer can follow

1. **Start from the platform text styles at their default size.** iOS Body is 17 pt, Footnote 13, Caption 1 12. Material Body Large is 16 sp. Do not invent bigger defaults. (Apple HIG typography, Android Compose M3)
2. **Meet accessibility by scaling, not by enlarging the default.** Use Dynamic Type, `sp` and zoom, then test at the largest sizes. (Apple HIG, Android `sp`, WCAG 1.4.4 Understanding)
3. **Use no more than three text sizes on a screen, and at most two big elements.** (NN/g visual hierarchy)
4. **Build hierarchy with weight and colour before size.** Two weights and two or three text colours are usually enough. (Refactoring UI, tip 1)
5. **Only page titles get full emphasis.** Everything else mixes up-pop and down-pop. (Erik Kennedy, rule 5)
6. **Design in black and white first. Add colour last, and only with a purpose.** (Erik Kennedy, rule 2)
7. **Keep colour to about 2 primary and 2 secondary colours.** (NN/g visual hierarchy)
8. **Separate groups with space, not boxes and borders.** (Refactoring UI, tip 4, Kennedy, rule 3)
9. **Use icons at the size they were drawn for, simple and schematic, with a visible label.** (Refactoring UI, tip 5, NN/g icon usability)
10. **Communicate; don't decorate.** Cut anything that is "decoration" only. (NN/g heuristic 8)
11. **Use metaphor only as a learning aid, never as styling.** Vignelli: "I don't believe that when you write dog the type should bark!" (NN/g skeuomorphism, Vignelli Canon)
12. **Use very few typefaces.** (Apple HIG typography, Vignelli Canon)
13. **Keep a 44×44 pt tap area, and let the visible element be smaller.** (Apple HIG accessibility)
14. **Copy the best existing work on purpose, then redesign it many times.** (Erik Kennedy, rule 7, Paul Graham)
15. **Hold honest, structured critiques and test a range of densities before choosing.** (Figma critiques post, Linear redesign post)

## The 15 clearest signs of junior work

1. **Everything is big.** Hierarchy is carried by size alone. (Refactoring UI, tip 1, Kennedy, rule 5)
2. **Body text above the platform default** for every user, the look the owner calls "designed for someone who wears reading glasses". (Apple HIG, WCAG 1.4.4 Understanding)
3. **Too many text sizes,** or more than two elements fighting to be largest. (NN/g visual hierarchy)
4. **Small-grid icons blown up** to 3–4× until they look "chunky". (Refactoring UI, tip 5)
5. **Elements "all relatively equal in size and color"** with no "breathing room". (NN/g visual hierarchy)
6. **Grey text on coloured backgrounds,** or contrast handled by guesswork. (Refactoring UI, tip 2)
7. **Too many colours in too many places.** (Erik Kennedy, rule 2, NN/g)
8. **Boxes, borders and cards around everything.** (Refactoring UI, tip 4)
9. **Visual puns and themed metaphors as styling** (type that tries to look like its subject). (Vignelli Canon, NN/g skeuomorphism)
10. **Ornament standing in for substance:** "swooshes and curlicues". (Paul Graham)
11. **Cartoon illustration used to add "personality"** to a plain layout. (AIGA Eye on Design)
12. **Icons with no labels,** or detailed, realistic icons. (NN/g icon usability)
13. **Elements that are slightly misaligned or unevenly sized.** These are the "tiny incongruencies" that senior reviewers spot. (Figma recruiter, Linear redesign)
14. **Trying to look clever or original instead of being right.** (Bierut, Monocle, Paul Graham)
15. **Only the default size was ever tested,** so the layout breaks at accessibility sizes, or it was built huge so that nothing breaks. (Apple HIG typography)
