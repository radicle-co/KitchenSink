# Synthesis: the needs across cooking, finding recipes, grocery lists and meal planning

Desk research, 2026-10-20, with a second round on 2026-10-21. It sums up 102 records in `cooking.md` (CK, 29),
`findingRecipes.md` (FR, 24), `groceryLists.md` (GL, 20) and `mealPlanning.md` (MP, 29). Read `README.md` for the evidence codes and the record
format. This file adds no new evidence. Every claim points to records, and the evidence is in those records.

## Method and limits

- **Nobody was interviewed or watched.** The evidence is about 30,000 app-store reviews of over 20 apps, forum threads,
  peer-reviewed studies (CHI, CSCW, DIS, ASSETS, TOIS and food-science journals) and consumer surveys.
- **The voices lean to complaints from people who already use an app.** Low-star reviews show what breaks. They do not
  show how common a problem is among all cooks, or what people who use no app need.
- **Reddit was largely blocked.** Two researchers reached it through an archive, and two did not reach it at all.
  Google Play was missing for two areas.
- **The sources are almost all English, and mostly from the US and UK.**
- **The four researchers counted "independent places" a little differently.** The meal-planning file is the strictest:
  it counts the whole App Store as one place. A record marked validated in one file is not always as strong as one in
  another. Read the evidence lines, not only the label.
- **Groups the evidence barely reaches:** non-English speakers, older cooks, low-income households (who are most
  affected by cost and waste), people who do not use apps at all, and children and teens who cook. Blind and low-vision
  cooks appear only through one or two studies.

## Thirteen themes across the four areas

Each theme shows up in more than one area. That is why they matter more than any single record.

1. **The recipe data is quietly wrong, and people stop trusting it.** Imports and AI rewrites change amounts and drop
   steps (CK-006, FR-011). Grocery lists lose items and the chosen serving size (GL-005). Diet and allergy filters let
   the wrong food through (FR-009, MP-006). People cannot tell before cooking whether a recipe will work, or whether a
   person even made it (FR-002, FR-013). Wrong data costs a meal, money, and for allergies, safety.
2. **The page around the recipe is hostile.** Stories, ads, pop-ups and crashes bury the recipe and cover the step in
   progress (FR-001, CK-005). The same pages fight screen readers and small text (FR-020, CK-016). This has the widest
   evidence of any theme.
3. **People need one safe, lasting home for recipes from everywhere.** Recipes live on websites, in social videos, in
   cookbooks and on family cards (FR-004, FR-005, FR-012, FR-021, FR-024). If a service closes, changes or starts
   charging, people lose their collections (FR-010, FR-019, GL-015). The fear of losing them stops people investing in any tool.
4. **Deciding what to eat is the daily burden.** People go blank and repeat the same few meals (FR-006, MP-003, MP-007).
   The real limits are tonight's time and energy, and everyone at the table. Diets, budget and the food on hand also
   limit the choice (FR-007, FR-008, FR-014, FR-015, MP-004, MP-005, MP-011).
5. **Many people do not want a weekly plan.** People start planning and stop (MP-016). Many decide in the moment from
   what is in the fridge, and say that works (MP-017). Not every night needs a recipe (MP-019). People want help
   without losing control (MP-018). A product that assumes a weekly plan serves only part of the need.
6. **Plans break, and recovery is the real job.** The day changes and the plan falls apart (MP-001). The store is out of
   the planned item, or the delivery substitutes it badly (GL-017, GL-010). Food bought for a plan spoils (MP-009).
7. **Knowing what is already at home matters, but tracking it is too much work.** It is the best-evidenced grocery
   problem (GL-001), and it drives waste (FR-015, MP-009, MP-010). But people who keep a pantry list usually give up
   (GL-008), and one study found that looking at stock first did not lower waste. Evidence on both sides is recorded in GL-001.
8. **Feeding a household is shared work that one person carries.** One person does the thinking (MP-002, GL-013).
   Others complain about the result but do not help decide (MP-015). Shared lists fall out of sync, and helpers buy the
   wrong thing (GL-002, GL-018). Sharing recipes is clumsy (FR-018).
9. **During cooking, people need the right fact at the right moment.** The amount where the step uses it (CK-001). What
   to do in parallel, and the whole job before starting (CK-003, CK-007). Their place kept, without touching a screen
   with dirty hands (CK-002, CK-008). Vague cues made checkable at their skill level (CK-004, CK-010).
10. **Recipes need to fit the cook, not the author.** Units, servings, real times, missing equipment and swaps (CK-011,
    CK-012, CK-013, CK-014, CK-019, FR-022, FR-023, MP-020, GL-004). Each problem is small. Together they make most
    recipes need work before they can be cooked.

11. **Cooking under time pressure overwhelms many cooks, and fear of failing keeps them from trying.** Round 2 found
    panic mid-recipe: pots boil over, garlic burns, steps get skipped, and some people give up (CK-021). One failed
    dish shakes confidence, and people fear wasting food and money (CK-023). Cooking for others raises the stakes
    (CK-024), and some people hide that they cannot cook (CK-026). When something goes wrong, they do not know why or
    whether it can be saved (CK-029). The evidence is strongest for beginners and anxious cooks. For experienced cooks,
    it comes back mainly with a new or fast recipe. No study measured how often it happens among ordinary cooks.
12. **Prep is a trade-off that recipes hide.** If cooks prep while cooking, something burns. If they prep everything
    first, it takes longer and makes more dishes (CK-022). The owner's hypothesis, that average cooks prep first because
    they do not trust themselves, is only partly supported. Self-distrust is the stated reason mainly for beginners,
    forgetful cooks and fast dishes. Many cooks get everything out, check that nothing is missing, then prep during the
    waits. Most prep first for new recipes and not for familiar ones. A recipe's stated time assumes one way without
    saying which (CK-013).
13. **Deciding dinner every day wears people down, and long plans do not fit real weeks.** The same decision comes back
    every day for years (MP-022), and the load falls mainly on women (MP-002). People cannot know on Monday what they
    will want on Thursday, or what the day will bring (MP-023). The planning session itself is a chore (MP-024). Plans
    made for an ideal week lead to waste and guilt (MP-025). People plan in seasons and drop off (MP-026). Of 23,283
    French planners, 79% plan only a few days ahead and 1% plan two weeks or more (MP-027). People keep planning only
    if they feel the payoff the same week: less stress at 5 pm, less takeaway, less waste (MP-028). Years of cooking for
    people who complain wear out the joy (CK-028), and after work even experienced cooks are too tired to start
    (CK-025).

### Counter-evidence that must be kept in view

- Most people say they enjoy cooking. In a Harris poll of 2,503 US adults, 79% enjoy it, but 49% only "when they
  have the time". In a study of working women, preparing food ranked in the middle of 22 daily activities, above
  housework and childcare (CK-027).
- 62% of US adults call themselves very or extremely confident cooks (Instacart 2025).
- In a study of 40,554 French adults, about six in ten non-planners do not find deciding what to cook a burden, and
  planners and non-planners ate almost the same nutrients (MP-003, MP-017).
- People differ a lot on repetition. Some happily eat the same meals almost every day, and others get bored within a
  month (MP-007).
- For some people, planning is the part of cooking they enjoy.
- The popular claim of "200 food decisions a day" does not hold up, and no study here measures food decision fatigue
  directly.
- What makes cooking feel good is recorded too: calm, order, a known dish, a finished meal and someone enjoying it
  (CK-027).

Round 2 changes to the ranking (2026-10-21):

- Need 4 now also covers the daily repetition of the decision, not only the single evening: MP-022, MP-024, MP-028.
- Need 6 now names the cause, not only the symptom: stay in control when the recipe moves faster than the cook. Add
  CK-021, CK-022 and CK-029.
- A new need sits between 4 and 5: **plan only as far ahead as the week allows.** Help needs to fit a few days, not
  a fixed week, and to survive a changed day without guilt (MP-023, MP-025, MP-026, MP-027).
- A new need sits after 6: **cook with confidence, including for others.** Know the result will be good, and know what
  to do when it is not (CK-023, CK-024, CK-026, CK-029).

## What this means for designers

These points follow from the evidence. They describe needs, not screens.

1. **Correctness is a design quality.** A beautiful screen with a wrong amount fails the cook. Show where a recipe
   came from, and make changes visible.
2. **Respect people who do not plan.** "What can I make tonight" deserves as much care as "plan my week". Most
   planners look only a few days ahead.
3. **Assume the plan will change.** The first-class moment is the change, not the plan.
4. **Design for the household, not the individual.** At least two people touch most lists and plans.
5. **Never make a person maintain data for the app's sake.** Pantry tracking shows the limit: people give up.
6. **The cook's safety is never a setting buried in a menu.**
7. **Keeping people's recipes safe and portable is part of the product.** Trust lost here is hard to win back.
8. **The pace of the recipe is the cook's problem to manage, so show it.** Make the prep trade-off and the waiting time
   visible before the cook starts, and serve both those who prep first and those who prep during the waits.
9. **Reduce the daily decision, not only the weekly one.** The burden is the repetition, and it lands on one person.

Owner principle (2026-10-21), not a finding: design for the cook who struggles, so that the help is comfortable or
unobtrusive for an experienced cook. Help is always there or within easy reach, and never in the way. The design brief
records it in `docs/design/uiRefreshWorkshop5/brief.md` §3b.

## Questions only real research can answer

Desk research cannot answer these. They need interviews, diary studies or watching people cook.

1. How common is each problem among all home cooks, including those who use no app?
2. What do people who cook in the moment, without a plan, actually do from fridge to plate?
3. How do households split the thinking work, and what are the other members willing to do?
4. What does "I trust this recipe" depend on: the source, the photos, the ratings, or a friend?
5. How do older cooks, low-income households, non-English speakers and blind or low-vision cooks experience each of the
   twelve needs?
6. Which of these problems do people pay to solve, and which do they accept as normal?

## How to keep this database alive

- Add new evidence to the existing record, and add new problems at the end of the right file with the next ID.
- When real interviews or tests happen, add them as `S` evidence and update the status and the ranking here.
- Date every change. Remove nothing. Mark a solved or disproved problem as such and say why.
